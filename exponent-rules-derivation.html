Three rules you can prove by counting

An exponent starts life as shorthand. a3 means three copies of a multiplied together — nothing more. Each tile below is one copy.

Part two · Where counting runs out

What could a0 possibly mean?

How many copies of a are multiplied in a0? None. So the tiles say the answer is nothing at all — zero.

Let’s test that against the rule you just proved.

The law under test ax · ay = ax+y
The law says
20 · 23 = 20+3 = 23 = 8
so whatever 20 is, it must satisfy
20 × 8 = 8
Part three

And a−1?

Multiplying something by itself negative once is not a thing you can do. But the law does not care what you can picture. It only cares about being obeyed.

The law under test ax · ay = ax+y
The law says
2−1 · 21 = 2−1+1 = 20 = 1
so whatever 2−1 is, it must satisfy
2−1 × 2 = 1
Part four

Half a copy of a

This time the product rule is not the one doing the forcing. The power rule is.

The law under test (ax)y = axy
The law says
(2½)2 = 2½ · 2 = 21 = 2
so whatever 2½ is, multiplying it by itself must give 2

The result

Eight rules, one decision

Only the top row was ever a discovery. Everything below it was forced: at each step there was exactly one value that let the law survive, and that value became the definition. Nobody voted on a0 = 1. It was the only option left standing.

Two conditions on the printed table finally make sense.

Why the table also needs a > 0, not just a ≠ 0

Most textbook tables print “a ≠ 0” above the whole list, and for the first seven rules that is enough. Fractional exponents need more. (−8)1/3 = −2 is perfectly fine, but (−4)1/2 asks for a number that squares to −4, and no real number does. Once you allow arbitrary fractional exponents on a negative base, the law you spent this lesson protecting stops holding. So the honest condition for the last two rows is a > 0.
